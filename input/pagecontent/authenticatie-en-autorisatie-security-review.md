This page lists potential security risks in the proposal [Vervolg harmonisatie: clientregistratie en mandatering](authenticatie-en-autorisatie-vervolg-mandaat-clientregistratie.html), and gives an overview of these risks for each mandate variant (M0–M5).

### Scope and method

The review looked for vulnerabilities in the proposal that are not already covered by the FAPI 2.0 Security Profile; FAPI 2.0 requirements are assumed to be implemented. It covers:

- **Residual risks (R1–R7):** risks in the proposal as a whole, after the measures described in [Findings addressed in the proposal](#findings-addressed-in-the-proposal).
- **Findings for M4 and M5 (F1–F9):** risks specific to the variants that sign or seal mandates in the LRZa.

Section numbers (§) refer to the proposal. Severities are qualitative: High, Medium or Low.

### Overview per mandate variant

The mandate variants are described in §2.2 of the proposal:

| Variant | Short description |
|---|---|
| **M0** | Service provider authenticates with the care provider's UZI key (current practice) |
| **M1** | Care provider signs a mandate with its UZI key; service providers store the mandates, send them in the token request and publish the source's mandate in the resource server's metadata |
| **M2** | Care provider seals a mandate with its European Business Wallet; stored and published as in M1 |
| **M3** | Care provider registers the mandate as an `OrganizationAffiliation` in the LRZa portal; checked in the local replica |
| **M4** | As M3, but one service provider holding the care provider's UZI key registers and signs the mandates |
| **M5** | As M3, but the care provider seals the mandate in the LRZa portal with its European Business Wallet |

#### Summary per variant

- **M0** has the largest exposure. The service provider holds the care provider's key, acts with all its rights, and cannot be distinguished from it. A compromise of the service provider affects all its care providers, and only revoking the UZI certificate stops misuse.
- **M1** binds the mandate firmly: the URA is in the signing certificate, `may_act` names the service provider, and expiry and a status list allow revocation. Its weak point is who holds the UZI key: if a service provider does, as is common in SaaS, it can sign its own mandates.
- **M2** has the strengths of M1 and gives evidence through a qualified seal. The link to the care provider depends on the KvK-to-URA mapping (F5), the service provider prepares the mandate (F4), and wallet delegation could let it seal its own mandate (F9).
- **M3** keeps keys away from service providers and, given the portal requirements, prevents self-mandating. Its weak point is that mandates are not signed: a compromise of the LRZa, the portal or a replica can add mandates that cannot be detected (R2), and identifiers in the LRZa can be changed (R3).
- **M4** adds signatures to M3, but the key-holding service provider has M0-like power and can mandate itself (F6, F7). It only blocks mandates that never existed, and only once F1–F3 are fixed. `HSYSADMIN-custodian` becomes the source of the power to issue mandates, which raises the impact of R6 and R7.
- **M5** offers the strongest protection against a compromise of the LRZa and gives evidence through a qualified seal, provided that F1–F5, F8 and F9 are fixed. Its weakest point is the KvK-to-URA mapping (F5), and it is not yet operational.

When several variants are accepted at the same time, the weakest accepted variant determines how well the system resists a compromise of the LRZa (F3).

The proposal recommends M4 in the short term, with M5 as the growth path. For M4, F1–F3 and F6–F8 need to be addressed; moving to M5 adds F4, F5 and F9, and accepting both during the transition combines their risks (F3).

### Findings addressed in the proposal

An earlier round of this review produced findings that have since been addressed in the proposal:

|---|---|

| Self-mandating via the LRZa | Mandates only through the portal, eHerkenning at least EH3, no chain authorization, notification and waiting period (§2.2, M3) |

#### R1. The purpose-code hierarchy widens mandates
A mandate for a purpose also covers its specializations, and the authorization server derives the required mandate code from the `purpose_of_use` in the request. In the code system, `TREAT` covers `ETREAT`, `BTG` (*break the glass*), `POPHLTH` and `CLINTRL`, among others; `PATRQT` covers `FAMRQT`, `PWATRNY` and `SUPNWK`. A service provider with only `TREAT-user` can therefore send a request with `purpose_of_use` `BTG` or `POPHLTH`. If the source applies more permissive rules for such purposes, for example in emergencies, the service provider gains a power the care provider did not knowingly grant. A finer code such as `ETREAT-user` can only grant less; it cannot exclude `BTG` for a holder of `TREAT-user`.

*Possible measure:* map purposes to mandate codes with an explicit list instead of the hierarchy. Exclude `BTG`, `POPHLTH`, `CLINTRL`, `FAMRQT`, `PWATRNY` and `SUPNWK` unless they are explicitly mandated, and have sources reject purposes without a mapping.

#### R2. Mandates in the LRZa are not signed
In M3, the authorization server relies on the `OrganizationAffiliation` in its replica; sealing by the LRZa was deliberately left out. Anyone who gains write access to the LRZa, the portal or the replication can therefore add mandates and request data on behalf of any care provider. Such forgeries cannot be told apart from genuine mandates afterwards, and in a dispute there is no verifiable evidence that a mandate existed. The notification and the overview in the portal come from the LRZa itself, so they do not reveal a compromise of the LRZa.

*Possible measure:* have the LRZa seal each mandate with a key in an HSM separate from the portal, and have authorization servers verify the seal. An alternative is a signed, append-only log of mandates. M4 and M5 address this risk with a signature or seal of the care provider itself, provided that F1–F5 are fixed. Without a measure, this is recorded as an accepted risk.

#### R3. Mandates rely on LRZa identifiers that service providers can change
Care providers and service providers are found in the LRZa through `Organization.identifier` and `partOf`. Service providers with management rights for a care provider can change these fields. In M2, a service provider can add its own KvK number to a care provider's `Organization` and then seal a mandate to itself with its own business wallet; the authorization server maps that KvK number to the care provider's URA through the LRZa and accepts the mandate. In M3, a sub-organization carrying another care provider's URA can make lookups ambiguous.

*Possible measure:* take URAs, KvK numbers and the mapping between them only from the authoritative registers (UZI register and Handelsregister), make them unchangeable through ITI-130-NL, and enforce uniqueness. Allow mandates only for top-level organizations with a URA from the register.

#### R4. The access token is not bound to the source care provider
The access token records the requesting care provider (`sub`) and the service provider, but not which source care provider it applies to. In the original memo, the source's UZI signature bound the token to the source; that binding no longer exists. Where an authorization server and resource server act for several care providers, a token issued for care provider B2 can be used for data of care provider B1. This bypasses a decision by B1, such as a withdrawn custodian mandate.

*Possible measure:* include the source care provider (`locations_organization_id`) and the purpose in the access token or the introspection response. Have the authorization server check that it acts for that care provider, and have the resource server restrict every request to that care provider.

#### R5. Client-id derivation and scope are not fully specified
The requirements in §2.4 exclude personal certificates and decide M0 by certificate type. Three points remain open:
- The format of the client id (KvK number, OIN or URA) and how it is compared with `participatingOrganization` in the LRZa.
- All server certificates of an organization with the same identifier lead to the same client id and receive all of that organization's mandates. A key stolen from another system of that organization therefore gives access to all its mandates.
- Which rights a client created on first use receives by default.

*Possible measure:* define the client id as a typed identifier (system and value) and compare both. Optionally let a service provider record in the LRZa which certificates or domain names it uses for these exchanges. Give clients created on first use no rights beyond what the mandate check allows.

#### R6. A generic custodian mandate allows taking over a source endpoint
`TREAT-custodian` is not bound to an endpoint or payload type, and an organization can have several valid endpoints for the same payload type. A service provider that a care provider has mandated as custodian for one small application, and that has write access in the LRZa, can register a BgZ or eOverdracht endpoint for that care provider. Requesting service providers accept that endpoint and send requests containing patient data to it. The risk is low because the attacker must already be mandated by the care provider.

*Possible measure:* have the LRZa allow at most one valid endpoint per organization and payload type, or allow a custodian mandate to be limited to an endpoint or payload type.

#### R7. `HSYSADMIN-user` grants write access, and code migration is undefined
The `user` role is defined as requesting and receiving data, but `HSYSADMIN-user` also allows updating the service provider's own resources, such as endpoints. A care provider that gives its service provider the `user` codes thereby unknowingly grants write access to its addressing data. The proposal replaces the current codes `lrza-careprovider-admin` and `lrza-endpoint-admin`, but does not define how. If holders of `lrza-endpoint-admin` receive `HSYSADMIN-custodian`, they silently gain write access to the whole `Organization`, including identifiers and contact details (see R3 and the notification in §2.2).

*Possible measure:* make `HSYSADMIN-user` read-only or drop it. Publish a one-to-one migration table with a narrow, endpoint-only code, and exclude `identifier`, `partOf` and `telecom` from what service providers may change.

### Findings for variants M4 and M5

M4 and M5 add a `Provenance` to each `OrganizationAffiliation` in the LRZa: signed with the care provider's UZI key by one key-holding service provider (M4), or sealed by the care provider with its European Business Wallet in the LRZa portal (M5). #### F1. Withdrawn mandates can be revived by replaying an old signature
The signature covers the content of the `OrganizationAffiliation`, including `id` and `active: true`, but not `Resource.meta`, so the version is not signed. There is no required expiry and no signed revocation. Deactivating a mandate in the portal is an unsigned change that only makes the old signature invalid, while old `Provenance` records remain and are replicated everywhere.

*Attack scenario:* a care provider withdraws a mandate in the portal. A party that can write that `OrganizationAffiliation` sends the old content with the old signature again, and the LRZa and every authorization server accept it as valid. That party could be a colluding service provider with management rights, the key holder in M4, or anyone who has compromised the LRZa. A compromised LRZa can also simply not distribute the deactivation. Reactivation does not trigger the notification and waiting period, which apply only to new mandates.

*Possible measure:*
- Require a signed `period.start` and a short signed `period.end`.
- Allow reactivation or a wider `code` only through the portal, or accept them only with a `Provenance` whose signing time is later than the last deactivation.
- Never reuse an `id` after deactivation or deletion.
- Treat every change to `active` as a new mandate, with notification and waiting period.
- To resist a compromise of the LRZa, sign revocations too: seal them with the wallet in M5, or use an append-only log sealed by the LRZa with a version number that verifiers check.

#### F2. The signature does not bind the care provider's URA or the service provider's identity
- `organization` and `participatingOrganization` may refer to a resource id only.
- In the [signing example](signing.html), the URA appears only in `identifier.assigner`, which the signer fills in.
- Resolving a resource id to a URA or KvK number goes through unsigned, modifiable LRZa `Organization` data.
- The signing page compares the certificate with `Provenance.signature.who`, which is outside the signed content.

*Attack scenario:* a compromised LRZa changes the KvK number on a service provider's `Organization`. Every signed mandate for that service provider then matches the attacker's `client_id`; for `TREAT-custodian` mandates this also routes patient queries to the attacker's endpoint. If a verifier compares the certificate's URA with `signature.who` or `assigner`, anyone with any UZI key can sign a mandate that points to another care provider's resource.

*Possible measure:*
- For mandates, require `organization.identifier` (the URA) and `participatingOrganization.identifier` (the typed client id) inside the signed content.
- Verifiers compare the certificate's URA (or the mapped URA in M5) and `subject_organization_id` with the signed `organization.identifier`, and the `client_id` with the signed `participatingOrganization.identifier`.
- Verifiers never use `Provenance.agent`, `Provenance.signature.who`, `assigner` or LRZa `Organization` data for these checks. Step 6 of the signing page should be corrected accordingly.

#### F3. Downgrade to unsigned mandates
Signing only protects against a compromise of the LRZa if every verifier rejects every unsigned mandate.
- **Mixed variants.** The proposal expects them: M4 first and later M5, while M0 remains usable. A verifier can only tell which variant applies from LRZa data, which an attacker would control.
- **The key holder's own mandate.** In M4, the key-holding service provider's management mandate is itself registered unsigned through the portal.
- **Conflicting rules.** The [Care Service Directory](csd.html#mutation-signing-optional) and the ITI-130-NL capability statement state that the LRZa SHALL NOT require a signature. Reopening ITI-130-NL for mandate codes without changing those texts lets unsigned mandates in again.
- **Combined risks.** Accepting both UZI signatures and wallet seals combines the risks of both.

*Possible measure:*
- Set one system-wide date after which unsigned mandates are rejected, without a per-care-provider flag in the LRZa.
- Also require signatures for `HSYSADMIN` codes.
- Amend the mutation-signing texts in the Care Service Directory and the ITI-130-NL capability statement.
- Submit mandates atomically, for example in a transaction Bundle with a client-assigned id, because the signature covers the stored id.
- Record which signer is allowed for each care provider in a source outside the LRZa.

#### F4. What is signed may differ from what was shown
The portal (M5) or the service provider (M2) prepares the mandate and passes the content, or only its hash, to the wallet. In remote-signing flows, a wallet often signs a hash it cannot display meaningfully, and one approval may cover several hashes.

*Attack scenario:* a compromised portal shows "TREAT-user for service provider P" but has the wallet seal a mandate for an attacker, possibly with extra codes, or slips in extra hashes. The result is a genuine qualified seal, and the notification comes from the same compromised LRZa.

*Possible measure:*
- The wallet itself shows the relevant mandate fields (mandating care provider, service provider, codes and period), taken from the data being sealed. A mandate format that the wallet understands makes this possible.
- One seal per explicit confirmation; no batches.
- Confirmation through a channel outside the LRZa.

#### F5. The KvK or EUID to URA mapping runs through modifiable LRZa data
Any legal entity can obtain a qualified seal from any EU trust service provider. The only link between a seal and a care provider is the mapping from KvK number or EUID to URA, which in the proposal runs through the LRZa `Organization` (see R3).

*Attack scenario:* a compromised LRZa, or a service provider with management rights, adds its own KvK number to a care provider's `Organization`. It then seals a mandate "care provider to attacker, TREAT-user" with its own legitimate wallet, and verifiers accept it.

*Possible measure:*
- Take the mapping only from authoritative sources outside the LRZa, such as the UZI register, or from a URA attestation issued by CIBG that is presented with the seal.
- Define how one KvK number with several URAs is handled.
- Make the mapping unchangeable through ITI-130-NL.
- Require a qualified electronic seal (QcType `eseal`, created on a qualified signature creation device) and an exact `organizationIdentifier` match, and reject natural-person certificates.

#### F6. Any UZI certificate that carries the care provider's URA can sign
Verifiers accept any valid UZI certificate whose URA equals the mandating care provider's. In current practice, several vendors often hold a UZI server certificate for the same care provider, and when the key holder changes, the old certificate is only replaced, not revoked. Any of these holders with write access through ITI-130-NL can mandate itself or a third party for any code. M4 does not require pass type `S` as M1 does, so a UZI card of one of the care provider's employees could also sign. The claim that only the key holder can mandate itself is therefore not enforced.

*Possible measure:*
- Accept only pass type `S`.
- Have the care provider register the one allowed signing certificate in the portal (eHerkenning, no chain authorization), identified by thumbprint.
- Require revocation of the old certificate when the key holder changes.

#### F7. The care provider cannot revoke against the key holder
- **Re-registration.** The key holder can re-register a mandate right after the care provider deactivates it, under a new id if it wants. The notification and waiting period only restart the cycle.
- **Withdrawing its management mandate is not enough.** Its existing signatures can still be submitted by another service provider with management rights.
- **Collateral damage.** Only revoking the UZI certificate stops it, and that breaks all of the care provider's mandates and M0 connections.
- **Wrong attribution.** Its signatures are cryptographically the care provider's, so in a dispute the care provider appears to have consented.

*Possible measure:*
- When the care provider deactivates a mandate in the portal, record a block on that combination of care provider, service provider and code that ITI-130-NL cannot override.
- When the key holder's management mandate is withdrawn, have the LRZa stop accepting its pinned certificate.
- State explicitly that ending the relationship requires revoking the UZI certificate.
- Include the service provider's identity in the signed content.
- Do not claim evidential value for M4 signatures.

#### F8. When certificate validity is checked is not defined
The signing time in the JWS header (`sigT`) is set by the signer, and M4 has no trusted timestamp. The proposal does not say whether certificate validity and revocation are checked at verification time or at signing time.
- **Checked at signing time:** if implementations accept "valid at `sigT`", a stolen, revoked or expired key can backdate `sigT` and keep producing valid mandates.
- **Checked at verification time:** renewing a certificate breaks all mandates at once, and there is no re-signing procedure.

*Possible measure:*
- For authorization decisions, require the certificate to be valid and not revoked at verification time: fail-closed, with the maximum OCSP or CRL age from §2.5.
- Use timestamps only as evidence.
- Have the LRZa reject a `sigT` that is more than a few minutes from its own time of receipt.
- Define how mandates are re-signed when a certificate is renewed.

#### F9. Wallet delegation lets a service provider mandate itself
The European Business Wallet lets an organization authorize representatives, including third parties, to operate its wallet. Small care providers are likely to delegate this to their EHR vendor. A seal identifies only the legal person, so a vendor sealing its own mandate cannot be detected. Unlike M3, M5 has no rule against chain authorization.

*Possible measure:*
- Require the person acting in the portal to be a representative of the care provider itself, shown by a representation attestation at a high assurance level, and refuse third-party delegations.
- Include the acting person, pseudonymized, in the sealed content.
